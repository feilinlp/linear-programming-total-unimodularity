## Slide 1 · Exhaustive tests · approximately 45 seconds

Now that we've seen how TU makes the problem simpler, how can we efficiently check whether a matrix is TU?

The simplest approach is to follow the definition directly, checking if every square submatrix has determinant -1, 0 or 1. However, as the formula shows, trying every choice of rows and columns gives us exponentially many submatrices to check in the worst case.

Our next attempt is Ghouila-Houri. For every subset of rows, we must be able to assign plus or minus signs so each column sums to -1, 0 or 1. Choosing a subset and its signs is equivalent to assigning each row -1, 0 or 1, where zero means leaving it out. With three choices per row, there are three to the m configurations, so checking them all is still exponential.

## Slide 2 · Structural recognition and handoff · approximately 45 seconds

To get a polynomial-time test, we turn to Seymour's approach, which breaks the matrix into smaller pieces connected through 1-, 2- or 3-sums.

These diagrams show the connections. A 1-sum has two zero blocks, so the pieces are separate. In a 2-sum, one connecting block has rank one and the other is zero. For a 3-sum, their ranks add up to two: either two rank-one blocks, as shown here, or one rank-two block and one zero block.

We repeat this on the smaller pieces, keeping the interface rows or columns that represent their connection, until we reach recognisable building blocks: network matrices, their transposes, or exceptional pieces. We then work back up: if both children are TU and the interface and signing conditions hold, their parent is TU too. Repeating this certifies the original matrix.
