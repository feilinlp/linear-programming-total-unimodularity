## Slide 4: Solving the LP (≈ 60 s)

*(Pick up from Yock Cheng: "…so now we can tell when A is TU.")*

Once A is TU, we still have to **solve** the LP. Here are three algorithms: how each works, and how fast it is.

**Simplex**, Dantzig, 1947, keeps moving to a **better neighbouring vertex**. It's fast in practice. / But Klee and Minty built a squashed cube where it visits **every** vertex: 2 to the n pivots. **Exponential** in the worst case.

So: is LP polynomial **at all**? / Khachiyan said **yes** in 1979, with the **ellipsoid method**. Cut the ellipsoid through its centre, keep the feasible half, and wrap it in a smaller one. **Polynomial**, but too slow to use.

**Interior point** methods, Karmarkar, 1984, get **both**. They move through the **inside** of the polytope: polynomial, and fast. / The catch: they may not stop at a vertex. We'll test that in E3.

## Slide 6 · 1/2: Testing the theory (≈ 28 s)

*(Pick up from Daryl after Slide 5.)*

So how will we **test** this? Four experiments, each checking a **prediction**.

**E1**: what happens when we **leave** TU? We take bipartite graphs and add odd-cycle edges one at a time. Close a path into a triangle and we get halves. Nemhauser–Trotter says **only ever halves**.

**E2**: how costly is **recognising** TU? We time brute force and Ghouila-Houri, which should be exponential, against the polynomial CMR library. `[→]`

## Slide 6 · 2/2: Testing the practice (≈ 28 s)

**E3**: does the **LP algorithm** matter? On TU graphs with tied optima, like C₄, the whole segment is optimal. Simplex returns an endpoint. Interior point without crossover returns the middle.

## Likely questions (short answers)

- **"Why is O(n^4 · L) polynomial but Ford–Fulkerson's O(mC) is not?"** L counts **bits**. C takes only log C bits, so O(mC) is exponential in the input size (Lecture 6). O(n^4 · L) is polynomial in n and L.
- **"Is there a polynomial pivot rule for simplex?"** None is known; it's an open problem. Spielman–Teng's smoothed analysis explains why it's fast in practice: tiny random perturbations make the expected time polynomial.
- **"Why not use the ellipsoid method?"** Its bound is polynomial, but each step is expensive and it needs close to its worst-case number of steps in practice. Its importance is theoretical: it only needs a **separation oracle** (Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver).
- **"Why can interior point return a fractional answer on a TU matrix?"** TU makes every **vertex** integral, not every optimal point. With tied optima, interior point converges to the centre of the optimal face, e.g. (½,½,½,½) on C₄. Crossover then moves it to a vertex.
- **"Does TU make the LP faster too?"** Yes. Its entries are in {0, ±1}, so by Tardos (1986) the LP is solvable in **strongly** polynomial time: the running time doesn't depend on b and c at all.
