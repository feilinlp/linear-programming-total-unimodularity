Our project asks when an LP relaxation can solve an integer optimisation problem exactly. We will study total unimodularity, using vertex cover as our running example.

About 40 seconds.

Vertex cover chooses the fewest vertices that touch every edge. The integer model uses zero or one, while the LP allows fractions.

On the triangle, one-half at every vertex satisfies all edge constraints and costs 1.5. An actual cover needs two vertices. On the four-cycle, choosing opposite vertices costs two, which is also the LP minimum.

Vertex cover is NP-hard in general, while LP has polynomial-time algorithms. What structure makes the relaxation exact, so that an integer optimum is available from the LP?

Background: all halves are also optimal on C₄. The displayed integer solution is one optimal solution, not the only one.

A matrix is totally unimodular if every square submatrix has determinant minus one, zero or one. The identity matrix is a simple example. The four-cycle's vertex-cover matrix is also TU. The triangle matrix has determinant two, so it fails the definition.

Next, we will see how the graph's edge constraints produce these matrices.

A square submatrix comes from choosing the same number of rows and columns. They need not be consecutive. The definition concerns every square submatrix, not just the full determinant.

Source: Jan Vondrák, Totally Unimodular Matrices, Stanford Math 233B, Lecture 3.

For C₄, write one constraint per edge. The first is x₁ plus x₂ at least one. Its coefficients give the highlighted matrix row: one, one, zero, zero. Doing this for every edge gives A₄x at least the all-ones vector.

We minimise the sum of the vertex values and keep each between zero and one. This is the same vertex-cover LP, written in matrix form.

The columns correspond to vertices v₁, v₂, v₃, v₄. The rows correspond to edges (1,2), (2,3), (3,4), (4,1). All vector inequalities apply coordinatewise.

We plan to study two results. Hoffman–Kruskal characterises TU through integer vertices for every integer right-hand side. The second result says a graph is bipartite exactly when its unsigned incidence matrix is TU.

4 · Solving the LP

Three LP algorithms: polynomial in theory vs fast in practice

Simplex

Dantzig

Walk to a better neighbouring vertex until none is better

Worst case: 2ⁿ − 1 pivotsFast in practice

Ellipsoid

Khachiyan

Cut through the centre, keep the feasible half, wrap it in a smaller ellipsoid

Polynomial: O(n⁴L)Slow in practice

Interior point

Karmarkar

Follow a path through the inside of the polytope towards the optimum

Polynomial: O(n³·⁵L)Fast in practice

n = variables, L = input size in bits; operation counts for m = O(n) constraints. Interior point may stop off a vertex when optima tie → tested in E3.

Once A is TU, we still have to solve the LP. Lecture 7 named three algorithms. Here's how each works, and how fast it is.

Simplex, Dantzig, 1947, keeps moving to a better neighbouring vertex. It's fast in practice. But Klee and Minty built a squashed cube where it visits every vertex: 2 to the n pivots. Exponential in the worst case.

So: is LP polynomial at all? Khachiyan said yes in 1979, with the ellipsoid method. Cut the ellipsoid through its centre, keep the feasible half, and wrap it in a smaller one. Polynomial, but too slow to use.

Interior point methods, Karmarkar, 1984, get both. They move through the inside of the polytope: polynomial, and fast. The catch: they may not stop at a vertex. We'll test that in E3.

So how will we test this? Four experiments, each checking a prediction.

E1: what happens when we leave TU? We take bipartite graphs and add odd-cycle edges one at a time. Fractional values should appear, but only halves, by Nemhauser–Trotter.

E2: how costly is recognising TU? We time brute force and Ghouila-Houri, which should be exponential, against the polynomial CMR library.

E3: does the LP algorithm matter? On TU graphs with tied optima, we run simplex and interior point, with and without crossover, and count the integral answers.

E4: if the LP is exact, should we just use it? We compare it with Dinic and Hopcroft–Karp. Same answer expected, but slower.
