When Is an LP Enough?

Total unimodularity and exact integer optimisation

Yee Hern · Yock Cheng · Feilin · Daryl

Our project asks when an LP relaxation can solve an integer optimisation problem exactly. We will study total unimodularity, using vertex cover as our running example.

About 40 seconds.

Vertex cover chooses the fewest vertices that touch every edge. The integer model uses zero or one, while the LP allows fractions.

On the triangle, one-half at every vertex satisfies all edge constraints and costs 1.5. An actual cover needs two vertices. On the four-cycle, choosing opposite vertices costs two, which is also the LP minimum.

Vertex cover is NP-hard in general, while LP has polynomial-time algorithms. What structure makes the relaxation exact, so that an integer optimum is available from the LP?

Background: all halves are also optimal on C₄. The displayed integer solution is one optimal solution, not the only one.

A matrix is totally unimodular if every square submatrix has determinant minus one, zero or one. The identity matrix is a simple example. The four-cycle's vertex-cover matrix is also TU. The triangle matrix has determinant two, so it fails the definition.

Next, we will see how the graph's edge constraints produce these matrices.

A square submatrix comes from choosing the same number of rows and columns. They need not be consecutive. The definition concerns every square submatrix, not just the full determinant.

Source: Jan Vondrák, Totally Unimodular Matrices, Stanford Math 233B, Lecture 3.

How Do We Check Whether a Matrix Is TU?

CS4234 Mini-Project · When is an LP enough?P2 · Yock Cheng

≈ 45 s · full script in script/P2.md

First check that entries lie in {−1, 0, 1}; otherwise a 1×1 submatrix already fails. Brute force chooses every k-row, k-column pair and checks its determinant exactly. One failure rejects the matrix; acceptance requires all sizes and choices.

For an m×n matrix, the number of nonempty square submatrices is Σ from k=1 to min(m,n) of binom(m,k)binom(n,k). The number of checks grows exponentially in the worst case. The bottleneck is enumeration, not the cost of one determinant. Early rejection can make individual non-TU instances quick.

Ghouila-Houri characterises TU by a signing condition for every row subset. Signs may differ between subsets. The displayed C₄ calculation is (+row 1)−row 2+row 3−row 4=(0,0,0,0), using P1's edge-row, vertex-column convention. It is an illustration, not a complete certificate.

For direct enumeration each row is omitted, positive or negative: 3 choices per row. Transposition can reduce the enumerated dimension. The exponential label applies to this implementation, not to TU recognition in general.

Sources: ETH Zürich, Combinatorial Optimization, Lecture 2; CMR TU recognition documentation. No measured results are shown.

A 1-sum has no connection between the diagonal blocks. A 2-sum has one rank-one off-diagonal connection. Low rank by itself does not certify TU, and child matrices can include interface rows/columns beyond the displayed diagonal blocks.

Seymour's theorem concerns regular matroids: graphic, cographic and R₁₀ building blocks under structured sums. At matrix level, recognition uses network matrices, transposes, exceptional forms and allowed transformations. Recursively find permitted separations, recognise the terminal pieces, and check the interface and signing conditions. This leads to a polynomial-time TU recogniser.

Recursion proceeds on each constructed child, including its required interface rows/columns. Stop when a child is recognised as a base class or a failure is detected. For valid signed sum constructions, TU of all children implies TU of the parent; apply this bottom-up to certify A. Testing only the diagonal blocks A₁ and A₂ is insufficient. CMR also verifies the signing, so regularity of the unsigned support alone is insufficient for the original signed matrix. The diagrams are schematic rather than actual CMR output.

Once A is TU, we still have to solve the LP. Here are three algorithms: how each works, and how fast it is.

Simplex, Dantzig, 1947, keeps moving to a better neighbouring vertex. It's fast in practice. But Klee and Minty built a squashed cube where it visits every vertex: 2 to the n pivots. Exponential in the worst case.

So: is LP polynomial at all? Khachiyan said yes in 1979, with the ellipsoid method. Cut the ellipsoid through its centre, keep the feasible half, and wrap it in a smaller one. Polynomial, but too slow to use.

Interior point methods, Karmarkar, 1984, get both. They move through the inside of the polytope: polynomial, and fast. The catch: they may not stop at a vertex. We'll test that in E3.

So when is TU useful? In two ways.

First, it explains algorithms we already know. Max-flow's matrix is a directed incidence matrix, so Lecture 6's integrality theorem follows. Bipartite matching, like our C₄, is TU, and with LP duality it gives König's theorem.

Second, it solves problems with no known shortcut. In shift scheduling each shift covers consecutive hours, so the matrix is TU and the LP alone gives the schedule.

To finish, here is how the pieces fit. Everything hangs on one property: if A is TU and b is integral, the LP optimum is the integer optimum.
