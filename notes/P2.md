# P2 · Recognising total unimodularity

## Presentation framing

Brute force and Ghouila-Houri introduce alternative ways to recognise TU and motivate structural recognition. They are not experimental baselines or implementation commitments. Our project will use Seymour-based recognition through CMR.

## Scope and notation

Use A ∈ ℝ^{m×n}: m rows, n columns. In the displayed C₄ example rows are edges and columns are vertices, as in P1. Only P2 slides were replaced; the other presenters' work was preserved.

## Brute-force test

Reject entries outside {−1,0,1}, then enumerate every nonempty square submatrix. An exhaustive determinant test requires exact arithmetic. Accept only when every determinant is −1, 0 or 1; one counterexample rejects.

Count: Σ_{k=1}^{min(m,n)} binom(m,k)binom(n,k) = binom(m+n,m)−1. For m=n this grows exponentially. A non-TU instance may reject early, so the worst-case comparison does not predict every individual runtime.

## Ghouila-Houri

For an integer matrix, TU is equivalent to: every subset of rows admits a ±1 signing whose sum is in {−1,0,1} in every column. A different signing can be used for each subset.

The slide illustrates all four C₄ rows with signs (+,−,+,−); the sum is (0,0,0,0). One successful subset does not establish TU. For an arbitrary subset of these cycle rows, its selected edges form paths (or the full even cycle); alternating edge signs along each component supplies a signing.

Direct enumeration has three states per row: omitted, positive, negative. A straightforward recomputation costs O(mn·3^m). TU is preserved by transposition, allowing the smaller dimension to be enumerated. CMR documents an optimised GH implementation with O((m+n)·3^{min(m,n)}). Do not attribute that tighter bound to an implementation without the required incremental computation.

## Seymour / CMR

Explain the process in three steps: recursively find structured separations; recognise terminal pieces; check the pieces, connections and signing to decide TU. Network matrices encode a graph with a spanning tree; remaining edges correspond to paths in that tree. The displayed leaf types are alternatives, not a decomposition every matrix must have. This is a structural recognition algorithm, not a search over every possible decomposition.

The slide now shows schematic block interfaces: 1-sum [[A₁,0],[0,A₂]], 2-sum [[A₁,abᵀ],[0,A₂]], and a distributed-rank 3-sum [[A₁,abᵀ],[cdᵀ,A₂]]. Nonzero outer products have rank one. A 3-sum can alternatively concentrate rank two in one off-diagonal block. These sketches illustrate connections, not sufficient TU conditions or complete child-matrix constructions. The connector letters are local vectors.

The slide is a conceptual illustration. Regular matroids decompose into graphic, cographic and R₁₀ pieces through 1-, 2- and 3-sums. Matrix forms require allowed transformations and signing; arbitrary block splitting is not a TU test. Explain the building-block intuition only. Use the CMR implementation, with Seymour's proof and reimplementation outside scope.

## Verified recursion and sum forms

Checked the diagrams against CMR documentation and Nägele et al., Definition 13, Theorem 15 and the following remark. The 3-sum shown is the distributed-rank form, not an exhaustive definition. Permutations and pivoting can be necessary. The sum operands include interface rows/columns; they are not merely A₁ and A₂.

Recursively recognise each child until a base case or a failure is reached. With valid signed sum conditions, all TU children imply a TU parent, and this implication propagates up the tree. Arbitrary joining of TU blocks need not preserve TU. CMR's signing checks are essential to testing the actual signed matrix rather than just regularity of its binary support.
